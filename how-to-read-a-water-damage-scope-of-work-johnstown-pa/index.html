How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Johnstown, PA

A glossary of the terms in a scope, what each means and what to ask when a line makes no sense.

☎ (814) 358-8169

By the Johnstown Water Damage Pros team

The scope is the contract in plain English

A scope of work lists what the restoration company will do, where and with what equipment. It is not the same as the price, though the price follows from it. Reading the scope carefully helps you understand what you are agreeing to and gives you a way to compare quotes fairly.

Scopes look technical, but the terms are not hard once you know them. This guide goes through the common ones.

Reading the affected areas

A good scope lists each room, the approximate square footage and the materials affected. It should say what is wet and what is being removed, such as the lower two feet of drywall in the kitchen. A scope that says only water damage in the house is too vague to be useful.

Compare the room list with your own walk-through. If a room that you know is wet is missing, ask about it. If a room that is dry is listed, ask why.

Reading the drying plan

The drying plan should say how many pieces of equipment are used, for how long and how progress is measured. The standard ANSI/IICRC S500 expects a documented drying goal. Ask what the goal is and how the readings will be recorded.

More on the process is on our structural drying page. If a scope has equipment but no mention of readings, you cannot tell when the job is done.

Reading the removal and rebuild

Removal items should explain what is discarded and what is saved. A flat line that says remove drywall with no area is incomplete. The rebuild, which includes new drywall, paint, flooring and trim, should be separate from the emergency work, since it is often priced and approved separately.

See wall and drywall water damage repair for how a drywall repair scope is typically laid out.

Line items that deserve a second look

A scope lists rooms, materials and tasks. Look for words like "allowance," "as needed" or "TBD," since these leave the final price open. A line that says "remove and replace drywall" with no square footage is hard to check. Ask for measurements and quantities wherever you can.

Make sure drying equipment is described with the number of units and the expected days. Equipment is a common source of surprise charges.

Do not sign until you understand it

You can ask the company to walk you through the scope line by line. If your insurer is involved, send the scope to your adjuster before work starts so there are no surprises. A company that is confident in its scope will not mind questions.

Keep a signed copy and ask for updates in writing if the scope changes during the job.

Compare two scopes the right way

If you have two scopes, line them up by room and task, not by total price. One may include more drying days or a different amount of tear-out. Ask each company to explain the difference and decide which matches the damage you can see.

Remember that a lower price with fewer drying days can end up costing more if materials stay wet.

Scopes and rebuilds in Johnstown

A rebuild scope in Johnstown may need a building permit. The city requires permits for repairs and renovations, though painting and flooring are exempt, and the Laurel Municipal Inspection Agency issues them at (814) 471-0424. A good scope says who is responsible for permits.

Older Johnstown houses may have plaster, old wiring and surprises behind walls. A line that allows for concealed conditions is reasonable. In a nearby town like Salix, ask the township office what applies to your address.

The same question applies in Seward, across the county line.

What to do when a line makes no sense

Ask. A good company can explain any line. If the answer is vague or defensive, ask for the line to be removed or rewritten. If the explanation makes sense, you have learned something and you can move on.

Keep a copy of the scope and any changes. If hidden damage is found and the scope changes, ask for the change in writing before work continues.

Standards and sources

The drying and water category terms used here come from ANSI/IICRC S500, the industry standard for professional water damage restoration. For claims, the Pennsylvania Insurance Department advises photographing damage before cleanup and keeping receipts.
