How to Shut Off Your Home Water Main in Johnstown, PA

Where the valve usually is, how to turn it and what to do while you wait for help.

☎ (814) 358-8169

By the Johnstown Water Damage Pros team

Know this before you need it

When a pipe bursts, every minute with the water running adds damage. Knowing where your main shutoff is and that it works is one of the best protections you have. Take a few minutes this week to find it, and make sure everyone in the house knows too.

This guide explains the usual places to look and how to deal with a stuck valve. Every house is different, so use it as a starting point.

Where to look

Main valves tend to be near where the line enters the house.

Types of valves

A gate valve has a round wheel handle that you turn clockwise to close. It can take several turns. A ball valve has a lever handle, and a quarter turn across the pipe closes it. Many older houses have gate valves that have not been used in years and may seize.

Turn it slowly and firmly. If it will not turn, do not force it with a wrench so hard that you break it. A broken valve makes the problem worse.

After the valve is closed

Open a faucet at the lowest point of the house to drain the line, and one at the highest to let air in. If the leak is still running, the problem may be on the street side of the valve. Turn off the water heater power or gas if it is draining, and keep power off near water.

Take photos, move belongings out of the water and call the plumber and the restoration line. See burst pipe water damage for what happens next.

If the valve is stuck or leaking

A valve that will not turn or leaks at the stem needs a plumber. Call the utility if you cannot shut the water off at all. In the meantime, if the leak is from an appliance or fixture, use the small valve under it or behind it.

Replace an old gate valve with a ball valve when you can. A ball valve is easier and more reliable in an emergency. It is a small job for a plumber.

What to do right after you turn it off

Once the water is off, open the lowest faucet in the house to drain the line. This relieves pressure and limits how much water can leak out of the broken spot. If the leak is near the water heater, turn off its power or gas as well.

Move valuables and furniture away from wet areas, take photos and then call the plumber and the restoration line. Do not step into standing water if any outlet or appliance is nearby.

Know your secondary shutoffs

Toilets, sinks, washing machines and the water heater each have their own small valves. Learning where they are means you can stop a small leak without cutting water to the whole house. Test them once a year, since old valves can stick.

Washing machine hoses deserve special attention. Braided steel hoses last longer than rubber, and many people close the valves when they go away for a trip.

Tell the whole household

Everyone who lives in the home, including teenagers and house sitters, should know where the main valve is. A written note taped near the panel or inside a utility cabinet helps. If you rent, ask the landlord where the shutoff is and write it down.

A two minute walkthrough on a calm day is much easier than a phone call in a panic.

Water service in the Johnstown area

The shutoff at the street, often called the curb stop, is generally the utility's to operate. In the City of Johnstown, the Greater Johnstown Water Authority can be reached at 814-533-4300. Ask them where your curb stop is and what to do if you cannot shut the water off inside.

Outside the city, your provider differs, and some homes in places like Nanty Glo and Elton may use private wells with a pressure tank and a different shutoff. Find out which you have before an emergency.

Homes in Sidman may differ again.

Practice once a year

Turn the valve off and on once a year to keep it from seizing, and check for drips. Label the valve with a tag. Show the household where it is and how it turns.

If your house has a washer, a water heater and an upstairs bathroom, you have more places for failure. See water heater leak cleanup for how a tank failure plays out.

Standards and sources

The drying and water category terms used here come from ANSI/IICRC S500, the industry standard for professional water damage restoration. For claims, the Pennsylvania Insurance Department advises photographing damage before cleanup and keeping receipts.
