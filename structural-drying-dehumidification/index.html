My house is damp and will not dry

Most people reach this page after noticing the signs, not the trade term. Walls that feel cool, a floor that is still damp days after a leak, a musty smell that returns and fans that never seem to catch up all mean the structure is holding water. Structural drying is the process of removing that moisture from framing, subfloor, drywall and concrete using controlled airflow and dehumidification.

It is also called water mitigation, the work that follows extraction and comes before repair. The goal is to reach a written dry standard so nothing is closed up wet.

Moisture mapping: finding the wet areas

Drying starts with a map. Moisture meters measure the actual moisture content in wood and drywall, and a thermal camera shows cool spots where water is held. The crew marks the boundary of the wet area, takes baseline readings and records them, then returns at each visit to compare.

This matters because water travels. A wet carpet near an exterior wall may have soaked the wall cavity, and a ceiling stain often traces back to a wet joist bay. Without a map, equipment goes in the wrong place and drying takes longer.

Water mitigation and the drying goal

Water mitigation is the early drying work that limits the damage. The standard the trade uses, ANSI/IICRC S500, calls for a documented drying goal, often set by measuring a dry reference material in the same house. When your wet materials match that reference, they are considered dry.

Drying usually takes a few days, but dense materials like hardwood, plaster and concrete take longer. Cool, damp weather slows evaporation, so the crew may raise the room temperature to help. Equipment can be noisy, and the house will feel warm and dry while it runs.

What dry actually means

A wall that feels dry is not necessarily dry. Drywall can feel fine while the stud behind it is wet. Meters read the inside, which is why daily readings are recorded in the scope.

Closing a wall too early traps moisture and invites mold, so repairs wait for the final readings. For rooms where drywall or carpet was removed, the next steps are covered in wall and drywall water damage repair and carpet water damage restoration.

Drying in Johnstown houses

Johnstown has a lot of older homes, and many have plaster walls, thick framing and basements, all of which dry more slowly than modern drywall. Ask the crew how they plan to deal with plaster and old lumber.

The weather adds to the challenge. A cool, damp spell after a storm can stall drying, as can a house that is shut up in winter. For help with a flooded lower level, see basement water damage cleanup. Homes in Salix and nearby towns often sit higher and cooler, which matters for drying time.

Safety checklist and standards

The drying and water category terms used here come from ANSI/IICRC S500, the industry standard for professional water damage restoration. For claims, the Pennsylvania Insurance Department advises photographing damage before cleanup and keeping receipts.

  • Do not use a household vacuum on standing water.
  • Treat sewage or flood water as contaminated and wear gloves and boots.
  • Leave the home and call 911 or the gas utility if you smell gas.
  • Wash hands before eating after any contact with wet materials.
